## 1. Foundational studies on synchronous movement and liking, rapport, cooperation, self-other overlap: effect sizes, meta-analyses, replication status

### Takeaway
Two meta-analyses (Rennung & Göritz 2016; Mogan, Fischer & Bulbulia 2017) conclude that moving in synchrony has a small-to-medium positive effect on prosocial behaviour, perceived bonding, social cognition and (weakly) positive affect. But the literature is **contested**: the behavioural effect shrinks to indistinguishable-from-zero when only experimenter-blind studies are analysed, and Atwood, Schachner & Mehr (2022) argue expectancy/demand effects could explain much of it; a 2023 rebuttal disputes this. Treat "synchrony increases felt closeness" as moderately supported, and "synchrony increases actual cooperation" as weaker.

### Cited Findings

**Hove & Risen (2009)** [Single paper, 3 experiments; widely cited; conceptually replicated]
- Citation: Hove, M. J., & Risen, J. L. (2009). It's all in the timing: Interpersonal synchrony increases affiliation. *Social Cognition*, 27(6), 949–961. — [DOI 10.1521/soco.2009.27.6.949](https://doi.org/10.1521/soco.2009.27.6.949)
- Design: participant finger-taps alongside an experimenter; the experimenter tapped to a synchronous metronome, an asynchronous metronome, or did not tap. In both of the first two studies the *degree* of synchrony predicted later affiliation (liking) ratings; a third experiment showed the affiliative effect was unique to *interpersonal* synchrony (i.e. not produced by synchrony with a non-social timekeeper). — [abstract as quoted in secondary summary](https://bakadesuyo.com/2010/04/does-how-you-move-affect-whether-people-like/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1521/soco.2009.27.6.949)
- Later literature summarises the result as being due to the *timing* of the movements rather than matching of movement form. — [Cardiff ORCA preprint citing Hove & Risen](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/154446/1/Affiliative%20Effects%20of%20Interpersonal%20Synchrony%20-%20PrePrint%20Manuscript.pdf)

**Wiltermuth & Heath (2009)** [Contested for behavioural cooperation]
- Citation: Wiltermuth, S. S., & Heath, C. (2009). Synchrony and cooperation. *Psychological Science*, 20(1), 1–5. — [paper PDF copy](https://lchc.ucsd.edu/mca/Mail/xmcamail.2008_12.dir/att-0255/synchrony.pdf)
- Three experiments: groups that moved in step (e.g. walking in step around campus) were more cooperative in subsequent economic games; in Experiment 1 participants who walked in step chose higher numbers in the first round of the weak-link coordination game. Authors propose synchrony strengthens group cohesion. — [paper PDF copy](https://lchc.ucsd.edu/mca/Mail/xmcamail.2008_12.dir/att-0255/synchrony.pdf); [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=62381)
- Experiment 1 sample: 30 participants (60% female, mean age 20) walking in groups of 3 (figure from a blog quoting the paper; small sample). — [Deric Bownds' MindBlog quoting the paper](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2009/02/synchrony-and-cooperation.html)

**Valdesolo & DeSteno (2011); Valdesolo, Ouyang & DeSteno (2010)** [Single studies]
- Citation: Valdesolo, P., & DeSteno, D. (2011). Synchrony and the social tuning of compassion. *Emotion*, 11(2), 262–266. — [DOI 10.1037/a0021302](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021302); [PDF copy](https://dericbownds.net/uploaded_images/Valdesolo.DeSteno.2011.pdf)
- Finding: after a rhythmic (tapping) synchrony manipulation, synchronous others were perceived as more similar to the self and evoked more compassion and more altruistic helping than asynchronous others in the same plight; authors argue "a primary function of synchrony is to mark others as similar to the self". — [PDF copy](https://dericbownds.net/uploaded_images/Valdesolo.DeSteno.2011.pdf)
- A secondary blog reports +26% perceived similarity and +20% compassion in the synchrony condition — **unverified against the paper**. — [blog summary](https://bakadesuyo.com/2011/12/do-we-like-people-more-if-they-move-the-way-w/)
- Valdesolo, Ouyang & DeSteno (2010, *J. Exp. Soc. Psychol.*) is cited in later work for synchrony increasing perceived similarity/closeness and joint-task performance; methods not confirmed here. — [citing paper (Cirelli et al.)](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/Cirelli_2014.pdf)

**Tarr, Launay, Cohen & Dunbar (2015); Tarr, Launay & Dunbar (2016)** — endorphin hypothesis [Supported for self-reported bonding; endorphin link is indirect; cooperation null]
- Citation: Tarr, B., Launay, J., Cohen, E., & Dunbar, R. (2015). Synchrony and exertion during dance independently raise pain threshold and encourage social bonding. *Biology Letters*, 11(10), 20150767. — [DOI 10.1098/rsbl.2015.0767](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2015.0767); [Oxford repository record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:a6d752e4-2d41-4abe-9344-4370c14f5d0a)
- 2×2 design (synchrony × exertion), 264 Brazilian adolescents dancing in groups; both factors independently raised pain threshold (a *proxy* for endogenous opioid activity, not a direct measurement) and in-group bonding; low-exertion but synchronised movement still had an effect. — [Oxford news release](https://www.ox.ac.uk/node/91542); [Culture.Case summary](https://culturecase.kdl.kcl.ac.uk/research/2016/01/dance-raises-your-pain-threshold-and-aids-social-bonding)
- Citation: Tarr, B., Launay, J., & Dunbar, R. I. M. (2016). Silent disco: dancing in synchrony leads to elevated pain thresholds and social closeness. *Evolution and Human Behavior*, 37(5), 343–349. — [DOI 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2016.02.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2016.02.004); [PMC4985033](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4985033)
- Full synchrony raised pain thresholds and self-reported closeness; partial synchrony and asynchrony did not; **synchronised dancers did not behave more cooperatively in an economic game**. — [PMC4985033](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4985033)

**Meta-analysis 1: Rennung & Göritz (2016)** [Robust overall estimate, but with a serious blinding caveat]
- Citation: Rennung, M., & Göritz, A. S. (2016). Prosocial consequences of interpersonal synchrony: A meta-analysis. *Zeitschrift für Psychologie*, 224(3), 168–189. — [DOI 10.1027/2151-2604/a000252](https://doi.org/10.1027/2151-2604/a000252); [PMC5137339](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5137339)
- 60 published and unpublished experiments comparing a synchrony condition with at least one control; covers motor synchrony and synchronised sensory stimulation ("motor-sensory interpersonal synchrony", MSIS); reports a medium effect on prosociality for both attitudes and behaviour. — [abstract record](https://ebusca.uv.mx/Record/pubmed-5137339); [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/year/2022/docId/97119)
- **Key caveat**: when restricted to experiments in which the experimenter was naive to condition, the effect on prosocial *behaviour* was not distinguishable from zero (as reported by Atwood et al. 2022). — [Atwood et al. 2022](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0pp1x14d/qt0pp1x14d.pdf?t=rxgdex)

**Meta-analysis 2: Mogan, Fischer & Bulbulia (2017)** [Robust overall estimate]
- Citation: Mogan, R., Fischer, R., & Bulbulia, J. A. (2017). To be in synchrony or not? A meta-analysis of synchrony's effects on behavior, perception, cognition and affect. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 72, 13–20. — [DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2017.03.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2017.03.009)
- Results (qualitative labels from the abstract): **medium** positive effect on prosocial behaviour; **small-to-medium** on perceived social bonding and on social cognition; **small** on positive affect. Larger groups increased the effects on prosocial behaviour and positive affect, but group size did not moderate bonding or social cognition. — [abstract record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4EzD1Rzl); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2017.03.009)
- Described in secondary sources as a meta-analysis of 42 studies with more than 4,000 participants. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/moving-in-sync-creates-surprising-social-bonds-among-people/); [Frontiers paper citing it](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.886639/full)

**The expectancy critique and the rebuttal** [Contested]
- Citation: Atwood, S., Schachner, A., & Mehr, S. A. (2022). Expectancy effects threaten the inferential validity of synchrony-prosociality research. *Open Mind*, 6, 280–290. — [DOI 10.1162/opmi_a_00067](https://doi.org/10.1162/opmi_a_00067); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0pp1x14d/qt0pp1x14d.pdf?t=rxgdex)
- Claims: most published studies do not adequately control for experimenter bias; multiple independent replication attempts with added controls failed to find the original effects; and naive participants who never performed synchrony *predicted* the pattern of published results (positive and null), so placebo/demand could mimic the effect. — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0pp1x14d/qt0pp1x14d.pdf?t=rxgdex); [record](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-e3ac731a427c410fa337f16850089113/Details)
- Rebuttal: Tunçgenç, B., Bamford, J. S., Fawcett, C., & Cohen, E. (2023). The synchrony-prosociality link cannot be explained away as expectancy effect: Response to Atwood et al. (2022). *Open Mind*, 7, 711–714. Argues the link is also observed in carefully blinded designs and in infants/young children, who are unlikely to hold such expectations. — [NTU repository record](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/52825); [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/89524?show=full)
- Independent failures to replicate exist in specific settings, e.g. a Music Lab-affiliated study reports failing to replicate earlier findings in an auditory-only context. — [UConn Music Lab accepted manuscript](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2022/03/Liking-and-Sync-3-1-22-Accepted-no-track-changes.pdf)

**Neural correlates** [Single study]
- Citation: Cacioppo, S., Zhou, H., Monteleone, G., et al. (2014). You are in sync with me: Neural correlates of interpersonal synchrony with a partner. *Neuroscience*, 277, 842–858. — [DOI 10.1016/j.neuroscience.2014.07.051](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2014.07.051); [Birmingham PDF](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/17851541/Quinn_You_are_in_sync_with_me_Neuroscience_2014.pdf)
- Total 100 participants across studies; the participant was the *referent* (did not have to align to the partner, who was in fact a computer algorithm); being synchronised-with increased perceived synchrony/affiliation, and fMRI showed synchrony > asynchrony activity in regions tied to social cognition, embodied cognition, self-other expansion and action observation (incl. left inferior parietal cortex). — [Birmingham PDF](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/17851541/Quinn_You_are_in_sync_with_me_Neuroscience_2014.pdf); [later paper summarising it](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11097257/)

**Synchrony with robots / artificial partners** [Mixed, several nulls; directly relevant to the robot]
- Henschel & Cross, "No evidence for enhanced likeability and social motivation towards robots after synchrony experience", *Interaction Studies* (2020): between-subjects, drawing-task synchrony with a robot; no evidence that synchronous participants liked the robot more or asked it more questions. — [Western Sydney record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/no-evidence-for-enhanced-likeability-and-social-motivation-toward/); [John Benjamins](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/is.19004.hen)
- "Synchronous motion predicts enhanced perceived human–robot teamwork", *Scientific Reports* (Dec 2025): video-based, 34 participants; a cohesion/teamwork-perception effect of synchrony, but no significant main effect on affiliation intention. — [PMC12800258](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12800258/)
- Lund Humanities Lab project: humans showed more interpersonal synchrony when mirroring a human than a robot; synchrony with the robot rose over time and was linked to higher comfort. — [Lund humlab](https://www.humlab.lu.se/research/user-projects/social-robots-and-bodily-synchronisation/)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] The most defensible claim for a design rationale is: "synchrony reliably shifts *self-reported* closeness/similarity by a small-to-medium amount in human-human lab studies; effects on costly cooperative behaviour are less certain, and effects toward robots are not established." Overclaiming "synchrony builds trust in the AI" is not supported.
- [Speculation] Cacioppo et al. (2014) is encouraging for the robot case: the feeling of affiliation arose even when the "partner" was secretly an algorithm that synchronised *to* the participant. But participants believed it was a person, so belief about partner humanity may be a boundary condition (consistent with the null in Henschel & Cross).
- The self-other "similarity marking" account (Valdesolo & DeSteno) and the endorphin account (Tarr/Dunbar) are distinct mechanisms. The endorphin account relies on exertion and sustained group movement, which a single clink does not provide; the similarity/attention account is the more plausible one for toasting.

### Gaps
- **Exact pooled effect sizes not verified.** From memory (UNVERIFIED): Mogan et al. 2017 covered 42 studies, N ≈ 4,327, with approximately r ≈ .28 (prosocial behaviour), r ≈ .21 (perceived bonding), r ≈ .17 (social cognition), r ≈ .11 (positive affect). Rennung & Göritz 2016 reported Hedges' g values in the "medium" range for both attitudes and behaviour, and moderators including intentionality of synchrony and comparison-group type. Check originals before quoting.
- A search summary attributed the "60 experiments" abstract to Mogan et al.; that abstract (PubMed 28105388 / PMC5137339) belongs to Rennung & Göritz. The 42-study figure for Mogan is from secondary sources only.
- Hove & Risen sample sizes and test statistics were not accessible. Wiltermuth & Heath Exp. 2–3 (the cups-and-singing paradigm, public-goods game) details and N not verified (one snippet mentions 96 participants in groups of 3 without identifying the experiment).
- The specific failed replication of Wiltermuth & Heath attributed to Schachner & Garvin (unpublished, cited in the meta-analytic literature) could not be located/verified.
- Publication-bias statistics (trim-and-fill, p-curve) for either meta-analysis were not retrieved.

## 2. Joint action literature: shared goals, mutual prediction, coordination smoothers, sense of joint agency ("we-agency")

### Takeaway
Joint action research treats a coordinated act like a clink not as "moving at the same time" but as two agents representing a shared goal, predicting each other's part, and actively making themselves predictable. The feeling that an action was "ours" is strengthened by tighter coordination, a predictable partner, and success of the joint outcome; whether it extends to machine partners is disputed.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Sebanz, N., Bekkering, H., & Knoblich, G. (2006). Joint action: bodies and minds moving together. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 10(2), 70–76. — [CEU record](https://research.ceu.edu/en/publications/joint-action-bodies-and-minds-moving-together/); [PDF copy](https://vanderwel.camden.rutgers.edu/files/Sebanz-Bekkering-Knoblich-2006.-Joint-action_Bodies-and-minds-moving-together.pdf)
  - Definition: joint action is "any form of social interaction whereby two or more individuals coordinate their actions in space and time to bring about a change in the environment". — [quoted in 2012 critique](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/09515089.2012.670905)
  - The review organises the mechanisms as joint attention, action observation, task sharing, action coordination and agency; joint attention creates a "perceptual common ground". — [CEU record](https://research.ceu.edu/en/publications/joint-action-bodies-and-minds-moving-together/)
- Citation: Vesper, C., Butterfill, S., Knoblich, G., & Sebanz, N. (2010). A minimal architecture for joint action. *Neural Networks*, 23(8–9), 998–1003. — [DOI 10.1016/j.neunet.2010.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neunet.2010.06.002); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20598504/)
  - The architecture has three building blocks: **representations** (of the joint goal and of one's own task; minimally, that there is a part one is not doing oneself), **processes** (monitoring and prediction), and **coordination smoothers** — modifications of one's own behaviour that make coordination easier, used where precise coordination is needed and information about the partner is limited. — [author page](https://www.butterfill.com/writing/vesper_minimal_2010/); [CEU record](https://research.ceu.edu/en/publications/a-minimal-architecture-for-joint-action/)
- Neural evidence that people represent the partner's part in advance (Kourtis, Sebanz & Knoblich line of EEG work):
  - Stronger anticipatory motor activation when anticipating the action of an interaction partner than that of a non-partner ("loner"). Kourtis, D., Sebanz, N., & Knoblich, G. (2010). Favouritism in the motor system: social interaction modulates action simulation. *Biology Letters*. — [Stirling repository PDF](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/bitstream/1893/28124/1/2010_Kourtis_etal_favouritism_BioLetters.pdf)
  - Planning a joint (vs individual) action modulates frontal P3a / parietal P3b and the late CNV, interpreted as representing/simulating the partner's action beforehand; this anticipatory activity correlated with joint performance. Kourtis, Sebanz & Knoblich (2013), *Social Neuroscience*, DOI 10.1080/17470919.2012.694823. — [Stirling record](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/28095?mode=full)
  - "We-representations": specifying the joint configuration in advance reduced planning ambiguity and sped action onset. Kourtis et al. (2019), *Neuropsychologia*, "Evidence for we-representations during joint action planning". — [CEU record](https://research.ceu.edu/en/publications/evidence-for-we-representations-during-joint-action-planning/); [PMC6667733](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6667733)
- Sense of joint agency:
  - Review: Loehr, J. D. (2022). The sense of agency in joint action: An integrative review. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*. — [DOI 10.3758/s13423-021-02051-3 (record)](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4NG5oeKl)
  - Ratings shift toward *shared* control with increased coordination between partners (Bolt et al., 2016), with a more predictable partner (Bolt & Loehr, 2017), and with success of the joint performance (Loehr, 2018). — [Frontiers in Psychology 2024 critical review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1331084)
  - Pacherie distinguishes self-agency from joint agency ("The phenomenology of joint action: self-agency vs. joint-agency", 2012); a later proposal separates "we-agency" (joint agency grows at the expense of self-agency) from "shared agency" (both stay high). — [Springer, Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-016-9465-z)
  - [Contested] A 2024 perspective, "Sense of agency in joint action: a critical review of we-agency" (*Frontiers in Psychology*), argues the we-agency concept should be abandoned for a more parsimonious account. — [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1331084)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] Features that should make a human–robot toast feel *shared*, mapped from the literature: (a) an explicit common goal known to both (the glasses meet); (b) the robot's movement must be *predictable* to the human (Bolt & Loehr) — smooth, legible approach trajectory rather than a sudden snap; (c) the human must contribute a real, necessary part (task sharing), not just hold still while the robot does everything; (d) a clear *successful joint outcome* (Loehr 2018) — the audible/tactile clink is a natural success signal; (e) joint attention before the act (eye contact / mutual orientation as perceptual common ground).
- [Speculation] "Coordination smoothers" translate directly into robot motion design: reduce movement variability, exaggerate the approach so intention is readable, and use a conventional signal (raising the glass, saying "kanpai") as a timing cue.
- [Speculation] The Kourtis "favouritism" finding implies the human's motor system anticipates a partner's action only when that agent is construed as an interaction partner — so framing and prior interaction history with the AI may matter as much as kinematics.

### Gaps
- Full text of Vesper et al. (2010) was not accessible; the specific list of coordination smoothers (commonly summarised as: making one's own behaviour less variable/more predictable, exaggerated/signalling movements, use of synchronisation, affordance structuring, task distribution) is from memory and UNVERIFIED in this session.
- Knoblich, G., Butterfill, S., & Sebanz, N. (2011), "Psychological research on joint action: Theory and data", *Psychology of Learning and Motivation*, 54, 59–101 — known for the distinction between *emergent* coordination (entrainment, affordances, perception-action matching) and *planned* coordination (shared task representations, joint goals). Not retrieved in this session; cite only after checking.
- Numerical results for Bolt et al. (2016), Bolt & Loehr (2017, *Cognition*), Loehr (2018) not retrieved. What the 2024 critical review says specifically about joint agency with machine partners was not retrievable (commonly reported: weaker implicit agency effects with computer partners) — UNVERIFIED.

## 3. How precise must timing be? Temporal windows, and anticipation/mutual adaptation versus exact simultaneity

### Takeaway
I found **no source giving a validated millisecond tolerance for the affiliative effect of synchrony**. What the evidence does support is that (a) affiliation scales continuously with the *degree* of synchrony rather than switching at a threshold, and (b) temporal *interdependence/contingency* and a moderately adaptive partner matter more than perfect simultaneity.

### Cited Findings
- In Hove & Risen (2009), affiliation was predicted by the *degree* of tapping synchrony (a graded relationship), and timing rather than form-matching carried the effect. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1521/soco.2009.27.6.949); [Cardiff preprint](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/154446/1/Affiliative%20Effects%20of%20Interpersonal%20Synchrony%20-%20PrePrint%20Manuscript.pdf)
- Bowsher-Murray, C., Jones, C. R. G., & von dem Hagen, E. (2023). Beyond simultaneity: Temporal interdependence of behavior is key to affiliative effects of interpersonal synchrony in children. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*. Children aged 4–11, online tapping tasks (68 observing a pair; 63 tapping with a partner). Both *simultaneity* and *regularity* raised perceived affiliation via perceived "togetherness"; conclusion: temporal interdependence — "which includes but is not limited to simultaneity" — drives perceived affiliation. **No affiliative effect appeared when children experienced the synchrony themselves** (only when observing). — [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/157418/); [paper PDF](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/157418/4/1-s2.0-S0022096523000450-main.pdf)
- Adaptive virtual partner paradigm: Fairhurst, M. T., Janata, P., & Keller, P. E. (2013), *Cerebral Cortex*. The virtual partner corrected 0/25/50/75/100% of the participant's tap asynchrony; *moderate* correction gave the best synchronisation, while an over-adaptive partner was experienced as erratic; brain activity differed with partner adaptivity. — [abstract record](https://soundhealth.ucsf.edu/node/586); [follow-up preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/625061v1.full.pdf)
- General sensorimotor timing limits for 1:1 tapping (not affiliation-specific): a motor lower limit of roughly 150–200 ms inter-onset interval and a perceptual upper limit around 1.8 s for perceiving a beat. — [Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2022 (literature review section)](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fnhum.2022.916551)
- In joint-action planning for a synchronous glass clink, participants represented the partner's upcoming action in advance in addition to their own (Kourtis et al. 2014; see §6) — i.e. coordination is achieved by prediction, not by reacting. — [CEU record](https://research.ceu.edu/en/publications/attention-allocation-and-task-representation-during-joint-action-/)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] For a toast, "synchrony" is really *spatiotemporal convergence on a single contact event* — a discrete joint action, not rhythmic entrainment. The relevant timing variable is whether both parties' movements are mutually contingent and arrive together, not whether two periodic streams are phase-locked. Rhythmic-synchrony thresholds may therefore not transfer.
- [Speculation] Design implication from Fairhurst et al.: the robot should adapt to the human's approach *partially* (track and meet the human's glass) rather than either ignoring the human (fixed trajectory, 0% adaptation) or over-correcting jerkily (100%); and it should also be predictable enough that the human can adapt in turn (mutual adaptation).
- [Speculation] Because the effect is graded, a slightly-late clink probably degrades rather than abolishes the experience; a robot that makes the human *wait* visibly or collides early is likely worse than a small symmetric asynchrony.

### Gaps
- No ms-level tolerance for the affiliative effect was found. From general knowledge (UNVERIFIED, not sourced here): in dyadic tapping, mean asynchronies are typically in the tens of milliseconds, and audio-tactile/visual simultaneity windows are on the order of tens to ~100+ ms depending on modality. These should not be cited as affiliation thresholds.
- Launay, Dean & Bailes's work on synchronisation with a virtual partner and trust/affiliation (which manipulated believed agency of the partner) was not retrieved.
- Whether anticipation matters *more* than simultaneity for affiliation in adults has no direct head-to-head test that I found; the Bowsher-Murray evidence is from children and from third-party observation.

## 4. Behavioral mimicry (chameleon effect) and affiliation; replication status

### Takeaway
The chameleon effect (unconscious mimicry occurs; being mimicked increases liking) is a classic finding, but the *causal* "being mimicked → liking/rapport/trust" link is **contested**: a preregistered virtual-reality replication found no effect, and methodological critiques point to an unresponsive control condition and likely overestimated original effect sizes. Mimicry is also a different mechanism from synchrony (form-matching with a lag vs. time-matching).

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Chartrand, T. L., & Bargh, J. A. (1999). The chameleon effect: The perception–behavior link and social interaction. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 76(6), 893–910. Three studies: mimicry of a confederate's mannerisms occurs nonconsciously; participants whose mannerisms were mimicked rated the interaction as smoother and liked the confederate more (Study 2); dispositional perspective-takers mimic more. — [Duke scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/658197)
- Preregistered failure to replicate: Hale, J., & Hamilton, A. F. de C. (2016). Testing the relationship between mimicry, trust and rapport in virtual reality conversations. *Scientific Reports*, 6, 35295. Study 1 found higher rapport toward mimicking avatars; the **preregistered Study 2 found no effects of mimicry on rapport, trust ratings or implicit trust behaviour**; authors conclude being mimicked does not always increase rapport or trust. — [DOI 10.1038/srep35295](https://doi.org/10.1038%2Fsrep35295); [PMC5064448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5064448)
- Methodological critique: a short research report in *Social Psychological Bulletin* argues the usual control condition is confounded (an *unresponsive* confederate), so lower liking in control — rather than mimicry itself — may drive the difference, and that earlier work "may have yielded an overestimated effect size or even false-positive results". — [Social Psychological Bulletin](https://spb.psychopen.eu/index.php/spb/article/download/13261/13261.pdf)
- Mimicry manipulations vary from 11 seconds to fifteen minutes across studies, making results hard to compare. — [Polish Psychological Bulletin 2023](https://journals.pan.pl//Content/127821/PDF/2023-01-PPB-05.pdf)
- Synchrony ≠ mimicry: in Hove & Risen the affiliative effect was due to timing rather than matching of form. — [Cardiff preprint citing Hove & Risen](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/154446/1/Affiliative%20Effects%20of%20Interpersonal%20Synchrony%20-%20PrePrint%20Manuscript.pdf)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] In a toast, both parties perform the *same* gesture (raise, extend, clink) at the *same* time, so the act combines form-matching and time-matching. Given the replication record, the rationale should lean on the synchrony/joint-action evidence and not on "the robot mimics you, therefore you like it".
- [Speculation] The Hale & Hamilton null was obtained with *virtual avatars* — the closest analogue to an artificial agent — which is a further caution for expecting mimicry-based rapport with a robot. Detected (noticed) mimicry is widely held to backfire, so overt mirroring by a robot is risky.

### Gaps
- Lakin, J. L., & Chartrand, T. L. (2003). Using nonconscious behavioral mimicry to create affiliation and rapport. *Psychological Science*, 14(4), 334–339 (affiliation goals increase mimicry), and Lakin, Jefferis, Cheng & Chartrand (2003) "The chameleon effect as social glue", *J. Nonverbal Behavior*, were not retrieved in this session; citations are from memory — verify before use.
- No verified meta-analytic effect size for mimicry → liking. A marketing blog attributes d ≈ 0.3 to a "Hale et al. 2020 meta-analysis"; I could not confirm such a paper exists — treat as unverified and do not cite.
- Sample sizes of Chartrand & Bargh's studies not verified (original studies were small by current standards).

## 5. Does a single brief synchronized event (a few seconds, one clink) produce measurable effects?

### Takeaway
**Evidence for a one-shot, seconds-long synchrony event producing bonding is thin to absent.** Every affiliation/cooperation study identified here uses sustained or repeated synchrony (tapping trials, walking, dancing, singing) lasting on the order of minutes, and I found no study isolating a single discrete synchronised act. Neither meta-analysis is known (from what I could access) to establish a minimum effective dose.

### Cited Findings
- The foundational paradigms are sustained: repeated finger-tapping alongside an experimenter (Hove & Risen 2009) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1521/soco.2009.27.6.949); walking in step around campus in groups (Wiltermuth & Heath 2009) — [paper](https://lchc.ucsd.edu/mca/Mail/xmcamail.2008_12.dir/att-0255/synchrony.pdf); tapping to songs (Valdesolo & DeSteno 2011) — [PDF](https://dericbownds.net/uploaded_images/Valdesolo.DeSteno.2011.pdf); group dancing (Tarr et al. 2015, 2016) — [Biology Letters](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2015.0767), [PMC4985033](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4985033).
- The endorphin-linked effects were obtained with whole-body group dance and were additive with physical exertion. — [Oxford news release](https://www.ox.ac.uk/node/91542)
- In the neighbouring mimicry literature, manipulations as short as 11 seconds have been used, but that literature's results are heterogeneous and of doubtful replicability. — [Polish Psychological Bulletin 2023](https://journals.pan.pl//Content/127821/PDF/2023-01-PPB-05.pdf)
- A targeted search for duration/"dose" moderators or single-event synchrony studies returned nothing relevant. — [search basis: Rennung & Göritz abstract lists no duration result](https://ebusca.uv.mx/Record/pubmed-5137339)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] A single clink should not be expected, on current evidence, to produce a *measurable* bonding effect through the rhythmic-synchrony mechanism. If a toast bonds people, the more plausible routes are (i) joint-action success and shared agency (§2), (ii) ritual/conventional meaning and shared attention, and (iii) the surrounding context (shared drink, eye contact, speech) — these are outside this note's scope and belong to the ritual/commensality literature.
- [Speculation] Design consequence: either extend the synchronised phase (a coordinated sequence: raise → pause/eye contact → "kanpai" in unison → approach → clink → drink together gives several seconds of mutually contingent movement and a vocal synchrony component), or rely on repetition across sessions, rather than expecting the 100 ms of contact to do the work.
- [Speculation] An honest framing for the project: the clink is a *punctuation mark* that makes a shared moment perceptible, not a proven bonding dose.

### Gaps
- Exact manipulation durations in each foundational study were not verified from full text (from memory, UNVERIFIED: typically ~1.5–10 minutes).
- Whether either meta-analysis coded duration as a moderator could not be confirmed.
- No study of dose-response for synchrony duration was found.

## 6. Is there any study that directly examines toasting or glass clinking as a synchronized or joint action?

### Takeaway
**Yes, one — but only as a motor-planning paradigm, not as a bonding study.** Kourtis, Knoblich, Woźniak & Sebanz (2014) used synchronously lifting and clinking (beer) glasses as their EEG joint-action task. I found **no study that measures affiliation, trust, cooperation or closeness as an outcome of toasting/clinking**, and no Japanese-language experimental study on kanpai as synchrony.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Kourtis, D., Knoblich, G., Woźniak, M., & Sebanz, N. (2014). Attention allocation and task representation during joint action planning. *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience*, 26(10), 2275–2286. — [DOI 10.1162/jocn_a_00634](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_00634); [Stirling record](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/26758?mode=full); [CEU record](https://research.ceu.edu/en/publications/attention-allocation-and-task-representation-during-joint-action-/)
  - Task: two participants planned and performed **joint actions (synchronously lifting and clinking glasses)**, unimanual individual actions (lifting and moving a glass *as if* clinking with another person), and bimanual actions (clinking two glasses oneself). — [Stirling record](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/520272)
  - Findings (abstract): early in planning, attention is divided between locations relevant for one's own part and locations relevant for the partner's part; later, participants represent in advance the partner's upcoming action in addition to their own, though not at an effector-specific level. — [CEU record](https://research.ceu.edu/en/publications/attention-allocation-and-task-representation-during-joint-action-/)
  - An APS Observer piece describes Sebanz's EEG clinking study as showing that brain activity when planning a clink with a partner resembled that when clinking two glasses by oneself (bimanual) — i.e. the partner's part is planned somewhat like one's own second hand. — [APS Observer, "Living in Harmony"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/living-in-harmony-the-dynamics-of-social-coordination)
- Japanese-language search (J-STAGE; terms 同期/同調/タッピング/親和/好意/協力行動) returned no experiment linking synchrony to liking/cooperation, and nothing on 乾杯. Nearest hits were tangential: an experimental demonstration of interpersonal body synchronisation in a competitive (sprint-like) setting (*Japanese Journal of Sport Psychology*, 2022) — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjspopsy/49/1/49_2021-2104/_article/-char/ja); a study in which being approached by a robot increased liking for it (*日本感性工学会論文誌*, 2008) — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjske2001/7/4/7_4_807/_article/-char/ja).

### Inferences
- [Speculation] Kourtis et al. (2014) is the single most directly citable paper for the robot: it shows that a human preparing a clink already holds a predictive representation of the partner's glass movement. For a robot partner, the open question is whether humans form the same "we-representation" — which depends on the robot's movement being predictable and on the human treating it as a partner (§2).
- [Speculation] The bimanual-like planning finding gives a nice design metaphor: a well-coordinated clink is planned as if the partner were one's own other hand; the robot should aim to be as predictable as that.
- The absence of any outcome study means claims that "clinking glasses creates connection" are, in experimental psychology, an extrapolation from adjacent literatures, not a demonstrated effect.

### Gaps
- Kourtis et al. (2014) sample size, the required temporal precision of the clink, and behavioural asynchrony data could not be retrieved (full text blocked).
- The APS Observer description comes from a search snippet only; the article could not be opened to confirm wording or which paper it refers to (most likely Kourtis et al. 2014).
- Japanese sources were searched once on J-STAGE via web search only; CiNii was not searched directly. A null result here is weak evidence of absence.
- Anthropological/sociological work on toasting as ritual (commensality, drinking rituals) was out of scope and not searched; it may contain observational rather than experimental evidence.
