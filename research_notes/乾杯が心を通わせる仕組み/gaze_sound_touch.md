# Sensory and nonverbal channels of the toast (gaze, sound, touch, voice, gesture) and social connection

## 1. Mutual gaze: affiliation, arousal, trust, joint attention; toast eye-contact norms; Japanese gaze norms

### Takeaway
Eye contact is the best-evidenced channel: it reliably raises autonomic arousal and attention and signals approach/affiliation, but only when the perceiver believes they are *being seen* by a live partner, and its *evaluation* is culture-dependent (Japanese observers rate direct gaze as less approachable than Finns do, despite identical physiological responses). The European "look into the eyes when clinking" rule is documented only in popular/etiquette sources; no academic analysis of it was found. The single toasting-specific experiment (robot says "skål") found mutual gaze beforehand significantly increased people's toasting back.

### Cited Findings
**General functions of gaze**
- [M] Kleinke's review concludes gaze serves several functions (providing information, regulating interaction, expressing intimacy, exercising social control, facilitating service tasks) and that more gaze is generally associated with liking and positive evaluation, though prolonged gaze can also signal threat/dominance depending on context. Kleinke, C. L. (1986). Gaze and eye contact: A research review. *Psychological Bulletin, 100*(1), 78–100. — [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.100.1.78](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.100.1.78)
- [M] Intimacy-equilibrium (affiliative conflict) theory: eye contact, physical proximity, topic intimacy and smiling jointly set an "intimacy" level; when people are placed closer together they reduce eye contact to compensate. Argyle, M., & Dean, J. (1965). Eye-contact, distance and affiliation. *Sociometry, 28*(3), 289–304. — [doi:10.2307/2786027](https://doi.org/10.2307/2786027)
- [M] Kendon's observational study of dyadic conversation: speakers look away when starting long utterances and look back at the partner as they finish; gaze functions as monitoring, regulatory (turn-yielding) and expressive signal. Kendon, A. (1967). Some functions of gaze-direction in social interaction. *Acta Psychologica, 26*, 22–63. — [doi:10.1016/0001-6918(67)90005-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0001-6918(67)90005-4)
- [M] "Eye contact effect": perceived direct gaze modulates concurrent/subsequent cognitive processing and activation of the social brain network; proposed "fast-track modulator" model with a subcortical route. Senju, A., & Johnson, M. H. (2009). The eye contact effect: mechanisms and development. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 13*(3), 127–134. — [doi:10.1016/j.tics.2008.11.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2008.11.009)
- [M] A live person's direct gaze (vs. averted) elicited greater relative left-frontal EEG activation (approach motivation) and larger skin conductance responses; these effects were absent for pictures of the same faces. Hietanen, J. K., Leppänen, J. M., Peltola, M. J., Linna-aho, K., & Ruuhiala, H. J. (2008). Seeing direct and averted gaze activates the approach–avoidance motivational brain systems. *Neuropsychologia, 46*(9), 2423–2430. — [doi:10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2008.02.029](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2008.02.029)
- [V] Live vs. video call vs. pre-recorded video: direct gaze increased skin-conductance arousal relative to averted gaze in the live and bidirectional video-call conditions but **not** when watching a mere video; positive facial (zygomatic EMG) reactions occurred in all three. Authors: "an experience of being seen is essential for the autonomic reactions." Hietanen, J. O., Peltola, M. J., & Hietanen, J. K. (2020). Psychophysiological responses to eye contact in a live interaction and in video call. *Psychophysiology, 57*(6), e13587. — [doi:10.1111/psyp.13587](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13587); [Tampere Univ. release](https://www.tuni.fi/en/news/eye-contact-activates-autonomic-nervous-system-even-during-video-calls)
- [V, caveat] A 2012 Tampere master's thesis supervised by Hietanen reported that the mere *belief* of being seen did not affect skin conductance, which partially cuts against the pure "being seen" account (peer-review status unverified). — [thesis PDF](https://trepo.tuni.fi/bitstream/10024/84221/1/gradu06415.pdf)
- [M] Eye contact with a humanoid robot (vs. averted gaze) elicited skin conductance responses, zygomatic activity and heart-rate deceleration qualitatively similar to those elicited by a human. Kiilavuori, H., Sariola, V., Peltola, M. J., & Hietanen, J. K. (2021). Making eye contact with a robot: Psychophysiological responses to eye contact with a human and with a humanoid robot. *Biological Psychology, 158*, 107989. — [doi:10.1016/j.biopsycho.2020.107989](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2020.107989) (DOI unverified)
- [M] Review of "watching eyes" effects: perceived direct gaze increases self-awareness, prosocial behaviour, and memory for context. Conty, L., George, N., & Hietanen, J. K. (2016). Watching Eyes effects: When others meet the self. *Consciousness and Cognition, 45*, 184–197. — [doi:10.1016/j.concog.2016.08.016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2016.08.016)
- [M] Preferred duration of mutual gaze averaged about 3.3 s (N ≈ 500, large individual variation); longer or shorter gaze felt less comfortable. Binetti, N., Harrison, C., Coutrot, A., Johnston, A., & Mareschal, I. (2016). Pupil dilation as an index of preferred mutual gaze duration. *Royal Society Open Science, 3*(7), 160086. — [doi:10.1098/rsos.160086](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160086)
- [V] A 2023 *Scientific Reports* dual eye-tracking study (as summarized by phys.org) found true eye-to-eye contact occupied only ~3.5% of interaction time, yet its duration predicted subsequent social behaviour (gaze following). — [phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2023-11-eye-to-eye-contact-rare-social-behavior.html)
- [V] Obstructed eye contact reduces emotional mimicry (Frontiers in Psychology 2022), consistent with eye contact supporting affiliation. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.970954](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.970954)

**Gaze as coordination signal in joint action / handovers**
- [V] Robot-to-human handover (PR2, 102 naive participants): people reached for the object significantly earlier when the robot gazed at the handover location (shared-attention gaze); a turn-taking cue (gaze to location then to face) was preferred at trend level. Moon, A., Troniak, D. M., Gleeson, B., Pan, M. K. X. J., Zheng, M., Blumer, B. A., MacLean, K., & Croft, E. A. (2014). Meet me where I'm gazing: How shared attention gaze affects human-robot handover timing. *Proc. HRI 2014*, 334–341. — [doi:10.1145/2559636.2559656](https://doi.org/10.1145/2559636.2559656); [PDF](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/spin/sites/default/files/local_pdfs/2014_MeetMeWhereImGazing.pdf)
- [V] Follow-up: continual robot gaze at the receiver's *face* made receivers reach even earlier than gaze at the handover position; gaze *transitions* (location ↔ face) were perceived as communicating timing better. Zheng, M., Moon, A., Croft, E. A., & Meng, M. Q.-H. (2015). Impacts of robot head gaze on robot-to-human handovers. *International Journal of Social Robotics, 7*, 783–798. — [doi:10.1007/s12369-015-0305-z](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12369-015-0305-z)
- [M] Human–human handover analysis: handovers have a what/when/where coordination structure, and gaze is one of the cues used to establish joint attention before transfer. Strabala, K., Lee, M. K., Dragan, A., Forlizzi, J., Srinivasa, S., Cakmak, M., & Micelli, V. (2013). Toward seamless human-robot handovers. *Journal of Human-Robot Interaction, 2*(1), 112–132. — [doi:10.5898/JHRI.2.1.Strabala](https://doi.org/10.5898/JHRI.2.1.Strabala)

**Toasting-specific**
- [V] Field study ("in the wild", public event): a robot serving water said "skål" (cheers). Many people lifted their glass, replied "skål" and drank; they did so **significantly more often when the robot had first established mutual gaze** before speaking. Toasting and a joke were both effective persuasive strategies. Fischer, K., Langedijk, R. M., Nissen, L. D., Ramirez, E. R., & Palinko, O. (2020). Gaze-speech coordination influences the persuasiveness of human-robot dialog in the wild. *ICSR 2020* (LNCS 12483). — [author proof (SDU)](https://www.sdu.dk/-/media/files/om_sdu/institutter/idk/human-robot+interaction_files/2020publication_fischer+et+al_gaze-speech+coordination.pdf) (author list, venue and exact statistics **unverified** — sdu.dk was unreachable; finding is from the search abstract)
- [V] Japanese conference poster on the motor side of kanpai: Eguchi, K. & Fukui, T. (2020). 乾杯動作における眼・手腕協調過程 (Eye-hand coordination when doing a toast). 日本認知心理学会第18回大会発表論文集, PT1_38. Three conditions (no partner / partner without eye contact / partner with eye contact). With no partner, higher AQ (autistic-trait) scores went with lower peak wrist velocity; this relation disappeared in both partner conditions. It measures kinematics, not bonding; abstract only. — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/cogpsy/2020/0/2020_63/_article/-char/ja); [CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390287363457828992)
- [V, non-academic] The eye-contact-when-clinking rule (Germany, France, Czech Republic etc., with the "seven years of bad sex/luck" sanction) and its "trust / anti-poison" rationale are documented in popular and etiquette media only; the poison explanation is treated as folklore. — [VinePair](https://vinepair.com/articles/eye-contact-during-cheers-explained/); [Futura-Sciences](https://www.futura-sciences.com/sciences/questions-reponses/histoire-vraie-raison-troublante-laquelle-on-trinque-regardant-yeux-c7g3-23930/)

**Japanese vs. Western gaze**
- [V] Akechi, H., Senju, A., Uibo, H., Kikuchi, Y., Hasegawa, T., & Hietanen, J. K. (2013). Attention to eye contact in the West and East: Autonomic responses and evaluative ratings. *PLoS ONE, 8*(3), e59312. With live faces, direct gaze produced stronger heart-rate deceleration, shorter looking times and higher subjective arousal than averted gaze in **both** Finnish and Japanese participants; but Japanese participants rated a face making eye contact as **angrier, less approachable and more unpleasant** than Finns did. Authors attribute cultural differences to display rules/norms, not physiology. — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0059312](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3596353/); [UTokyo summary](https://www.u-tokyo.ac.jp/en/utokyo-research/research-news/japanese-find-eye-contact-makes-others-unapproachable/index.html)

### Inferences
- For a robot, the arousal/affiliation benefit of gaze probably requires that the human *feels seen* (contingent, responsive gaze toward the person's eyes at the right moment), not merely a face pointed forward. Kiilavuori et al. suggest a robot's eyes can trigger this.
- A toast compresses a handover-like gaze sequence: look at partner (address / "are we doing this?") → look at glasses (shared attention for the spatial rendezvous) → optionally back to partner at/after contact. Handover studies imply gaze at the rendezvous point improves *timing*, gaze at the face improves *engagement/readiness*; a transition between both communicates timing best.
- For Japanese users, a brief gaze (order of 1–3 s, cf. Binetti's ~3.3 s preferred duration in a UK sample) at the "kanpai" moment is safer than a sustained European-style stare, given Akechi et al.'s evaluative findings. This is an inference; no study has measured gaze during Japanese kanpai.
- Argyle & Dean predict that as glasses (and bodies) come close, people tolerate *less* eye contact; glancing down at the glasses at the moment of the clink is equilibrium-consistent and also functionally needed for aiming.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed observational study of where people look during human–human toasts in any culture was found; the European rule is etiquette lore. Whether Japanese kanpai involves glass-directed rather than eye-directed gaze is undocumented academically.
- No study isolates whether eye contact *during a clink* increases closeness. Fischer et al. is the nearest (gaze → compliance with a toast invitation), and its exact statistics could not be retrieved.
- Uono & Hietanen (2015, PLoS ONE, "Eye contact perception in the West and East") is relevant to Japanese/Finnish differences in what counts as eye contact, but its findings were not verified this session.
- Kendon & Ferber (1973), "A description of some human greetings" (distance salutation → approach with gaze aversion → close salutation with mutual gaze) would be the classic structural reference for greeting gaze; it is a book chapter with no DOI and was not verified this session.

## 2. Sound: jointly produced sound, joint agency, the clink itself, Spence's drinking-sound work, the "five senses" claim

### Takeaway
There is solid evidence that sounds matter to the drinking experience (Spence) and decent evidence that making sound/music together promotes prosociality and that a jointly caused tone is processed as "ours"; but **no peer-reviewed study of the glass-clink sound** (acoustics-as-experience, pleasantness, or social effect) was found, and the "clinking adds hearing so all five senses participate" story has no scholarly source.

### Cited Findings
**Joint sound-making and bonding**
- [M] 4-year-olds (N = 96, unverified) who engaged in joint singing/dancing/instrument play subsequently helped a peer and cooperated more than children who did a matched non-musical joint activity. Kirschner, S., & Tomasello, M. (2010). Joint music making promotes prosocial behavior in 4-year-old children. *Evolution and Human Behavior, 31*(5), 354–364. — [doi:10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2010.04.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2010.04.004)
- [M] fMRI (N = 18, unverified): drumming in synchrony with a partner activated the caudate (reward), and participants then picked up more "accidentally" dropped pencils for the in-sync partner than for an out-of-sync partner; caudate activity predicted helping. Kokal, I., Engel, A., Kirschner, S., & Keysers, C. (2011). Synchronized drumming enhances activity in the caudate and facilitates prosocial commitment – if the rhythm comes easily. *PLoS ONE, 6*(11), e27272. — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0027272](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0027272)
- [M] Tapping in synchrony with an experimenter increased liking of the experimenter. Hove, M. J., & Risen, J. L. (2009). It's all in the timing: Interpersonal synchrony increases affiliation. *Social Cognition, 27*(6), 949–960. — [doi:10.1521/soco.2009.27.6.949](https://doi.org/10.1521/soco.2009.27.6.949)
- [M] Theory/review: music-making bonds via self–other merging and endorphin release. Tarr, B., Launay, J., & Dunbar, R. I. M. (2014). Music and social bonding: "self-other" merging and neurohormonal mechanisms. *Frontiers in Psychology, 5*, 1096. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01096](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01096)

**Jointly caused sounds and sense of (joint) agency**
- [V] When two people act together to cause a tone, both initiator and responder show intentional binding (implicit agency marker) of equal size, although only the initiator reports explicit agency — interpreted as automatic formation of a "we" agentic identity. Obhi, S. S., & Hall, P. (2011). Sense of agency and intentional binding in joint action. *Experimental Brain Research, 211*, 655–662. — [doi:10.1007/s00221-011-2675-2](https://mijn.bsl.nl/article/10.1007/s00221-011-2675-2)
- [V] Auditory N1 attenuation for a tone that two people had to coordinate to produce: larger delays between the two partners' button presses reduced attenuation — people distinguish own vs. partner contribution at the sensorimotor level, and **tight temporal coordination matters**. Loehr, J. D. (2013). Sensory attenuation for jointly produced action effects. *Frontiers in Psychology, 4*, 172. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00172](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00172/full)
- [V] Pairs pressing keys together to produce a tone: binding was weaker in joint action when temporal coordination was demanding, but equal for independent and joint (complementary) actions when that demand was removed, supporting "we-agency". Jenkins, M., Esemezie, O., Lee, V., Mensingh, M., Nagales, K., & Obhi, S. S. (2021). An investigation of "We" agency in co-operative joint actions. *Psychological Research, 85*, 3167–3181 (author list/pages unverified). — [doi:10.1007/s00426-020-01462-6](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00426-020-01462-6)
- [M] Pairs producing tone sequences reported stronger *joint* agency when coordination was mutual (bidirectional adaptation) and more successful. Bolt, N. K., Poncelet, E. M., Schultz, B. G., & Loehr, J. D. (2016). Mutual coordination strengthens the sense of joint agency in cooperative joint action. *Consciousness and Cognition, 46*, 173–187. — [doi:10.1016/j.concog.2016.10.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2016.10.001) (DOI unverified)

**Sound in the drinking experience (Spence)**
- [V] Spence, C., & Wang, Q. J. (2015). Sensory expectations elicited by the sounds of opening the packaging and pouring a beverage. *Flavour, 4*, 35. Reviews evidence that opening and pouring sounds set expectations about a drink (carbonation, temperature, viscosity) and asks whether drinks have a "signature sound". No section on clinking was surfaced. — [doi:10.1186/s13411-015-0044-y](https://flavourjournal.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s13411-015-0044-y)
- [M] Spence, C. (2015). Eating with our ears: assessing the importance of the sounds of consumption on our perception and enjoyment of multisensory flavour experiences. *Flavour, 4*, 3. Argues sound is the "forgotten flavour sense". — [doi:10.1186/2044-7248-4-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/2044-7248-4-3)
- [M] Louder / high-frequency-boosted fizz sounds make a carbonated drink seem more carbonated. Zampini, M., & Spence, C. (2005). Modifying the multisensory perception of a carbonated beverage using auditory cues. *Food Quality and Preference, 16*(7), 632–641. — [doi:10.1016/j.foodqual.2004.11.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2004.11.004) (DOI unverified)
- [M] Review of glassware/container effects (shape, weight, material, colour) on beverage perception. Spence, C., & Wan, X. (2015). Beverage perception and consumption: The influence of the container on the perception of the contents. *Food Quality and Preference, 39*, 131–140. — [doi:10.1016/j.foodqual.2014.07.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2014.07.007)
- [V] Systematic review: audition makes a relevant contribution to taste perception. Guedes, D., et al., Crossmodal interactions between audition and taste: A systematic review and narrative synthesis. — [repository PDF](https://dspace.uevora.pt/rdpc/bitstream/10174/35136/1/Guedes_Crossmodal%20interactions%20between%20audition%20and%20taste-%20A%20systematic%20review%20and%20narrative%20synthesis.pdf)

**Impact-sound perception (what a "glass clink" tells the ear)**
- [M] Listeners identify the material of struck objects largely from decay/damping; glass and metal (low damping, long ringing) are distinguished from wood/plastic (high damping). Klatzky, R. L., Pai, D. K., & Krotkov, E. P. (2000). Perceiving material from contact sounds. *Presence, 9*(4), 399–410. — [doi:10.1162/105474600566907](https://doi.org/10.1162/105474600566907); Giordano, B. L., & McAdams, S. (2006). Material identification of real impact sounds. *JASA, 119*(2), 1171–1181. — [doi:10.1121/1.2149839](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2149839) (both DOIs unverified)

**"Five senses" claim**
- [V] No scholarly source was found for the claim that clinking exists to add hearing so that all five senses take part in drinking; the only explicit hit was a line of film dialogue, and popular articles list it alongside the (debunked) poison theory as folk etymology. — [IMDb quote page](https://imdb.de/title/tt0255653/quotes/); [ScienceABC popular article](https://www.scienceabc.com/eyeopeners/why-do-people-clink-their-glasses-before-drinking-a-toast)
- [V] Industry (not research) testimony that the ring of crystal is treated as part of a glass's appeal (Waterford designers). — [Civilian Global](https://civilianglobal.com/?p=11116)

### Inferences
- The clink is a single sound that neither party can produce alone and whose occurrence proves spatial-temporal rendezvous succeeded. By analogy to joint-agency studies, an immediate, clearly jointly caused sound should support a "we did that" experience; Loehr (2013) and Jenkins et al. (2021) suggest this depends on the sound following both parties' movements tightly.
- The sound must be *causally credible*: a real glass-on-glass ring (long decay, high pitch = "glass" per impact-sound work) emitted at the contact point. A speaker-played clink that is late or spatially displaced risks breaking both multisensory unity (section 6) and joint agency. A robot gripper that damps the glass (firm rubber grasp near the bowl) will deaden the ring; holding by the stem/base preserves it. (Engineering inference from damping acoustics, not a tested social effect.)
- Music/drumming evidence concerns *sustained rhythmic* synchrony over minutes; a single clink is a one-shot event, so transfer is uncertain.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed research on the acoustics, pleasantness or hedonic/social effect of the glass clink was found. Spence's corpus covers opening, pouring, fizz, ice and background music, not clinking (as far as could be determined).
- No study compares toasts with vs. without audible clink (e.g. plastic cups, air-toast, formal no-touch toasts with fine crystal) on enjoyment or closeness.
- Historical origin of clinking: only folklore/popular sources; the International Handbook on Alcohol and Culture reportedly describes toasting as a vestige of libation ritual, but this was seen only via a secondary quote (unverified).

## 3. Touch and mediated touch: interpersonal touch, handshake / high-five / fist-bump, contact through objects

### Takeaway
Interpersonal touch is linked to affiliation, compliance and cooperation, but the field has notable replication/integrity problems, and the clink is not skin-to-skin touch at all — it is a brief object-mediated impact felt through the glabrous palm/fingers. No study treats glass-to-glass contact as social touch; the nearest evidence is (a) tool-mediated vibration sensing, (b) haptic coupling through jointly held objects as a communication channel, and (c) mixed evidence on technology-mediated and robot touch.

### Cited Findings
**Interpersonal touch and affiliation**
- [M] Review: interpersonal touch conveys emotion, increases compliance and liking, and modulates bonding; the authors also flag how little controlled lab work existed and discuss mediated/virtual touch. Gallace, A., & Spence, C. (2010). The science of interpersonal touch: An overview. *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 34*(2), 246–259. — [doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2008.10.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2008.10.004)
- [M] "Midas touch": a waitress's brief touch on the hand or shoulder when returning change raised tips (roughly 12% no touch vs. ~17% hand / ~14% shoulder; N = 114 diners; figures unverified). Crusco, A. H., & Wetzel, C. G. (1984). The Midas touch: The effects of interpersonal touch on restaurant tipping. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 10*(4), 512–517. — [doi:10.1177/0146167284104003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167284104003)
- [V, replication/integrity caveat] A large share of later touch→compliance field studies come from Nicolas Guéguen. Brown & Heathers raised concerns about his work in 2017; Sage has issued expressions of concern on touch papers including "Touch, Awareness of Touch, and Compliance with a Request" (2002) and "Touch, Compliance, and Awareness of Tactile Contact" (2007), and five further Guéguen papers were retracted in 2025. — [Retraction Watch 2023](https://retractionwatch.com/2023/03/28/bust-size-and-hitchhiking-author-earns-five-expressions-of-concern); [Retraction Watch 2025](https://retractionwatch.com/2025/03/03/nicolas-gueguen-five-studies-bust-size-hitchhiking-retracted/)
- [M] NBA study: touch (fist bumps, high fives, chest bumps, etc.) coded from one early-season game per team in 2008–09 (294 players, 30 teams; unverified) predicted better individual and team performance later in the season, controlling for early performance, status and pre-season expectations; mediated by cooperation. **Correlational.** Kraus, M. W., Huang, C., & Keltner, D. (2010). Tactile communication, cooperation, and performance: An ethological study of the NBA. *Emotion, 10*(5), 745–749. — [doi:10.1037/a0019382](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019382)
- [M] fMRI of *observed* (animated) business encounters: a handshake preceding interaction increased positive evaluations (competence, interest in doing business) and nucleus accumbens activity and reduced the negative impact of avoidance behaviour (N = 18, unverified; observers, not handshakers). Dolcos, S., Sung, K., Argo, J. J., Flor-Henry, S., & Dolcos, F. (2012). The power of a handshake: Neural correlates of evaluative judgments in observed social interactions. *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience, 24*(12), 2292–2305. — [doi:10.1162/jocn_a_00295](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_00295)
- [M] Handshaking before negotiation signals cooperative intent and improves joint outcomes. Schroeder, J., Risen, J. L., Gino, F., & Norton, M. I. (2019). Handshaking promotes deal-making by signaling cooperative intent. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 116*(5), 743–768. — [doi:10.1037/pspi0000157](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000157)
- [M] Affective ("pleasant") touch is carried by C-tactile afferents tuned to slow stroking of **hairy** skin; glabrous skin of the palm is served by fast Aβ discriminative afferents. Löken, L. S., Wessberg, J., Morrison, I., McGlone, F., & Olausson, H. (2009). Coding of pleasant touch by unmyelinated afferents in humans. *Nature Neuroscience, 12*, 547–548. — [doi:10.1038/nn.2312](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2312); McGlone, F., Wessberg, J., & Olausson, H. (2014). Discriminative and affective touch: Sensing and feeling. *Neuron, 82*(4), 737–755. — [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2014.05.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.05.001)
- [V] Pre-registered meta-analysis (137 studies meta-analysed + 75 reviewed): touch interventions benefit physical and mental health; **object/robot touch gave similar physical benefits to human touch (0.56 vs 0.51) but smaller mental-health benefits (0.34 vs 0.58)**; small-study bias and impossibility of blinding noted. **Contact through objects / mediated touch**
- [M] When a hand-held object strikes something, the transient is signalled by fast-adapting type II (Pacinian) afferents that pick up vibrations transmitted through the object; such discrete contact events are key control points in manipulation. Johansson, R. S., & Flanagan, J. R. (2009). - [M] People can accurately localize where an impact occurred on a hand-held rod; the tool's vibration pattern is processed in somatosensory cortex as if the tool were an extension of the body. Miller, L. E., Montroni, L., Koun, E., Salemme, R., Hayward, V., & Farnè, A. (2018). - [M] Dyads jointly controlling an object amplify their forces to create a haptic information channel for coordination ("haptic coupling"). van der Wel, R. P. R. D., Knoblich, G., & Sebanz, N. (2011). (2006). - [M] Mediated social touch reviews: Haans, A., & IJsselsteijn, W. (2006). - [V, null result] Robot-initiated touch on the shoulder during a scary film: "merely simulating a human touching action with the robot's limbs is insufficient to elicit physiological, emotional, and behavioral responses in this specific context and with this amount of participants." Willemse, C. J. A. M., Toet, A., & van Erp, J. B. F. (2017). **Robot greeting-contact rituals**
- [V] A handshake from the robot Vizzy (45 participants) increased perceived warmth, animacy, likeability and willingness to help the robot. Avelino, J., et al. (2018). The power of a hand-shake in human-robot interactions. *IROS 2018*. — [CMU Portugal record](https://cmuportugal.org/publications/the-power-of-a-hand-shake-in-human-robot-interactions/)
- [V] Human–robot hand-clapping / high-five work with Baxter (Fitter & Kuchenbecker): facial reactivity made the robot seem more pleasant and energetic, whereas *physical* reactivity made it seem less pleasant, energetic and dominant in that design — contact responsiveness is not automatically positive. — [IEEE Spectrum report](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-high-fives-help-us-get-in-touch-with-robots); related paper [Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2018.00085/epub)
- [V] Online studies: observers judged a robot that touched a person's shoulder as more trustworthy and comforting but also more inappropriate. — [arXiv:2411.12549](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.12549)

### Inferences
- **Structural comparison (researcher's analysis, not from a single source).** Handshake, high-five, fist bump and clink share: (1) mutual approach to a rendezvous point negotiated on the fly, (2) a brief symmetric contact that neither can complete alone, (3) a discrete multisensory "completion" event, (4) immediate separation. They differ in: contact medium (skin vs. object), duration (handshake sustained with grip/force exchange; fist bump, high-five and clink are impacts of well under a second), sound (high-five and clink are loud by design; handshake silent), and intimacy/hygiene (clink keeps bodies apart — the object acts as proxy, making it available between strangers and across status lines where skin touch is not; consistent with Argyle & Dean's equilibrium logic). The clink is closest to the fist bump/high-five family (impact type) plus an instrument-like sound.
- Because the clink reaches the hand as a brief vibration through a rigid object onto glabrous skin, it should engage discriminative FA-II/Pacinian pathways (Johansson & Flanagan; Miller et al.), **not** the CT affective-touch system. Its social meaning is therefore likely carried by what the impact *signifies* (successful joint action, partner's physical presence and compliance), not by tactile pleasantness per se.
- The felt impact carries information about the partner: its timing, force and "give" are haptic evidence that a real, responsive agent is on the other side (cf. haptic coupling). For a robot this argues for real physical contact with human-like, slightly compliant impact dynamics rather than stopping short; a hard, rigid or mistimed impact may read as mechanical or threatening (and risks breakage).
- Touch-effect sizes in the literature should be discounted: Midas-touch style effects are small, context-dependent, and partly rest on work under integrity clouds; robot touch has documented null results.

### Gaps
- No study of object-mediated collisions (glass clink, bumping bottles, "cheers" with cans) as social touch was found. No psychophysics of what the hand feels in a clink.
- No experiment comparing real clink vs. air-toast (no contact) on closeness, with humans or robots.
- No controlled comparison of fist bump vs. high-five vs. handshake on rapport was found; the popular fist-bump literature is mostly about hygiene (e.g. Mela & Whitworth 2014, *American Journal of Infection Control* — not verified this session).
- Whether Packheiser et al.'s pooled estimates are affected by flagged Guéguen studies is unknown.

## 4. Synchronized vocalization ("Kanpai!" / "Cheers!" in unison)

### Takeaway
Lab and field studies report that speaking, chanting or singing in unison increases cooperation, affiliation and fast bonding among strangers, and meta-analyses find small-to-medium effects; but the effect on *behaviour* may shrink to zero when experimenters are blind, and all evidence concerns sustained synchrony (minutes), not a single shouted word. No study of the toast call itself was found, apart from Fischer et al.'s observation that people answer a robot's "skål".

### Cited Findings
- [M] Three experiments: participants who walked in step, or sang/moved in synchrony (e.g. to "O Canada"), later cooperated more in economic games (weak-link, public goods) than asynchronous or control groups, even at personal cost; positive emotion was not required. Wiltermuth, S. S., & Heath, C. (2009). Synchrony and cooperation. *Psychological Science, 20*(1), 1–5. — [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02253.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02253.x)
- [M] "Ice-breaker effect": in adult-education classes followed over ~7 months, singing classes bonded faster than craft/creative-writing classes at the first time point; by the end closeness was equal — singing accelerates, rather than uniquely creates, bonding. Pearce, E., Launay, J., & Dunbar, R. I. M. (2015). The ice-breaker effect: singing mediates fast social bonding. *Royal Society Open Science, 2*(10), 150221. — [doi:10.1098/rsos.150221](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.150221)
- [M] Groups reciting words in synchrony (vs. asynchronously) showed better memory, more affiliation/cooperative behaviour in subsequent tasks; i.e. **verbal** synchrony without music or movement is sufficient. von Zimmermann, J., & Richardson, D. C. (2016). Verbal synchrony and action dynamics in large groups. *Frontiers in Psychology, 7*, 2034. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02034](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02034) (article number/DOI unverified)
- [M] Synchronous ("joint") speech is easy for untrained people: pairs reading together achieve mean asynchronies of a few tens of ms (≈40 ms) with little practice. Cummins, F. (2003). Practice and performance in speech produced synchronously. *Journal of Phonetics, 31*(2), 139–148. — [doi:10.1016/S0095-4470(02)00082-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0095-4470(02)00082-7) (figure and DOI unverified)
- [M] Group singing raised pain thresholds (endorphin proxy) and closeness, including in very large choirs. Weinstein, D., Launay, J., Pearce, E., Dunbar, R. I. M., & Stewart, L. (2016). Singing and social bonding: changes in connectivity and pain threshold as a function of group size. *Evolution and Human Behavior, 37*(2), 152–158. — [doi:10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2015.10.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2015.10.002) (DOI unverified)
- [V] Meta-analysis: synchrony has a medium-sized effect on prosocial behaviour, small-to-medium effects on perceived bonding and social cognition, small effect on positive affect; group size moderates prosocial-behaviour and affect effects. Mogan, R., Fischer, R., & Bulbulia, J. A. (2017). To be in synchrony or not? A meta-analysis of synchrony's effects on behavior, perception, cognition and affect. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 72*, 13–20. — [PubMed/record](https://www.pubmed.gov/28105388/) (link from search; DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2017.03.009 unverified)
- [V, replication caveat] Meta-analysis: medium effect of interpersonal synchrony on prosocial attitudes and behaviour, moderated by intentionality and experimenter effects; **restricted to experiments with a blind experimenter, the effect on prosocial behaviour was not distinguishable from zero.** Rennung, M., & Göritz, A. S. (2016). Prosocial consequences of interpersonal synchrony: A meta-analysis. *Zeitschrift für Psychologie, 224*(3), 168–189. — [doi:10.1027/2151-2604/a000252](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/97119/2151-2604_a000252.pdf)
- [V, replication caveat] Atwood, Schachner & Mehr argue expectancy effects (participants' and experimenters' beliefs that synchrony bonds) threaten the validity of the synchrony–prosociality literature, and that replications with stronger bias controls have failed. "Expectancy effects threaten the inferential validity of synchrony-prosociality research," *Open Mind* (2022). — [PMC9987344](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9987344/)
- [V] Pharmacological field experiment: blocking μ-opioid receptors (naltrexone) reduced social bonding in a religious ritual, supporting the endorphin account of ritual bonding. Charles, S. J., et al. (2020). Blocking mu-opioid receptors inhibits social bonding in rituals. *Biology Letters, 16*, 20200485. — cited in [British Academy blog](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/blog/why-humans-love-alcohol/) (DOI 10.1098/rsbl.2020.0485 unverified)
- [V, toasting-specific] People spontaneously reply "skål" and raise their glass when a robot utters "skål", more so after mutual gaze. Fischer et al. (2020). — [author proof](https://www.sdu.dk/-/media/files/om_sdu/institutter/idk/human-robot+interaction_files/2020publication_fischer+et+al_gaze-speech+coordination.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Kanpai!" is a one-shot, ~0.5 s unison utterance, usually cued by a leader's call (音頭). Its plausible functions are (a) a *synchronization signal* that aligns everyone's glass-raising and the clink timing, and (b) a minimal dose of vocal unison. The bonding literature's dose is minutes of rhythmic activity, so (b) is a weak analogy; (a) is functionally obvious but unstudied.
- For a robot: speaking the word *together with* the human (overlapping, within a few tens of ms rather than turn-taking after the human finishes) is what the synchrony literature would recommend; this requires the robot to predict the human's onset or to lead with a prosodic lead-in ("kan—pai!") that the human can entrain to. Human joint-speech performance (~40 ms) sets a demanding target; perceptual tolerance for vocal asynchrony in this setting is unknown.
- Given expectancy concerns, part of the benefit may come simply from the shared understanding that "we are doing the ritual properly" rather than from synchrony mechanics per se — which still argues for reproducing the expected form.

### Gaps
- No study of unison toast calls (kanpai, cheers, prost, skål) and bonding was found; no data on typical asynchrony or on call-and-response vs. unison formats.
- Nuciforo (2013), "Russian toasting and drinking as communication ritual," *Russian Journal of Communication*, was surfaced as the only peer-reviewed article on toasting as a communication ritual, but it is an ethnography of communication (not experimental) and was not read; DOI unverified.
- Whether a robot voice in unison with a human produces any bonding effect is untested as far as found.

## 5. Gesture and approach: raising/offering an object toward another person; proxemics

### Takeaway
Raising a glass toward someone is an object-directed, other-directed reach that enters the partner's near space. Motor-cognition research shows that movements with social intent are kinematically distinct and readable, and that co-actors exaggerate movements to make them predictable; proxemics shows that reaching to arm's-length distance crosses into "personal" space reserved for acquaintances. None of this has been studied for the toast gesture except the Eguchi & Fukui kinematics poster.

### Cited Findings
- [M] Hall's proxemic zones (US middle-class sample): intimate (to ~45 cm), personal (~45–120 cm), social (~1.2–3.6 m), public (beyond). Clinking requires both arms to extend so the glasses meet inside personal distance. Hall, E. T. (1966). *The Hidden Dimension*. Doubleday. (book; no DOI) — background summarized in Sorokowska et al. below.
- [M] Preferred interpersonal distances vary across cultures (N = 8,943, 42 countries; unverified) for strangers, acquaintances and intimates. Sorokowska, A., et al. (2017). Preferred interpersonal distances: A global comparison. *Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology, 48*(4), 577–592. — [doi:10.1177/0022022117698039](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022117698039)
- [M] Proximity is part of the intimacy equilibrium: closer approach is compensated by reduced gaze. Argyle & Dean (1965). — [doi:10.2307/2786027](https://doi.org/10.2307/2786027)
- [M] Social intention changes reach-to-grasp kinematics: reaching for an object in order to hand it to / cooperate with another person differs measurably from the same reach done alone. Becchio, C., Sartori, L., Bulgheroni, M., & Castiello, U. (2008). The case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde: A kinematic study on social intention. *Consciousness and Cognition, 17*(3), 557–564. — [doi:10.1016/j.concog.2007.03.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2007.03.003) (DOI unverified)
- [M] Sensorimotor communication theory: in joint action, people deviate from the most efficient trajectory to make their action more discriminable/predictable for the partner ("signaling"). Pezzulo, G., Donnarumma, F., & Dindo, H. (2013). Human sensorimotor communication: A theory of signaling in online social interactions. *PLoS ONE, 8*(11), e79876. — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0079876](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0079876); empirical support e.g. Sacheli, L. M., et al. (2013). Kinematics fingerprints of leader and follower role-taking during cooperative joint actions. *Experimental Brain Research, 226*, 473–486. — [doi:10.1007/s00221-013-3459-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-013-3459-7) (DOI unverified)
- [M] Robot motion that is *legible* (intent-expressive) differs from merely predictable/efficient motion; legible motion lets observers infer the goal earlier. Dragan, A. D., Lee, K. C. T., & Srinivasa, S. S. (2013). Legibility and predictability of robot motion. *Proc. HRI 2013*, 301–308. — [doi:10.1109/HRI.2013.6483603](https://doi.org/10.1109/HRI.2013.6483603)
- [M] Handovers are negotiated through a coordinated what/when/where structure with approach, reach and transfer phases. Strabala et al. (2013). — [doi:10.5898/JHRI.2.1.Strabala](https://doi.org/10.5898/JHRI.2.1.Strabala)
- [V, toasting-specific] In kanpai movements, presence of a partner changed how autistic-trait level related to wrist peak velocity (relation present alone, absent with a partner) — i.e. the toasting reach is socially modulated. Eguchi & Fukui (2020). — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/cogpsy/2020/0/2020_63/_article/-char/ja)
- [V, toasting-specific] A companion robot for solitary drinkers (KAIST) proposes clinking glasses to make drinking enjoyable and to pace intake; authors note almost every drinking occasion in South Korea involves clinking; preliminary user study with 20 participants. Jung, Y., et al. (2023). Enjoy, but moderately!: Designing a social companion robot for social engagement and behavior moderation in solitary drinking context. *Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact. (CSCW2)*. — [PDF](https://ic.kaist.ac.kr/files/papers/jung2023enjoy.pdf); [ACM CSCW blog](https://medium.com/acm-cscw/how-can-a-social-companion-robot-support-enjoyable-but-moderate-solitary-drinking-d0983a508376) (author list/venue details unverified)
- [M] Performing ritualized gestures before consuming food/drink increased enjoyment and savoring, via greater involvement (four experiments; not about toasting or social rituals specifically). Vohs, K. D., Wang, Y., Gino, F., & Norton, M. I. (2013). Rituals enhance consumption. *Psychological Science, 24*(9), 1714–1721. — [doi:10.1177/0956797613478949](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613478949)

### Inferences
- The raise is an *invitation with a legible goal*: presenting the glass at mid-height, tilted toward the partner and pausing, declares "meet me here". A robot should use a legible (slightly exaggerated, smoothly decelerating) raise and leave the final closing distance partly to the human, so the contact is experienced as mutual (cf. mutual coordination → joint agency, Bolt et al. 2016).
- Meeting halfway matters symbolically and proxemically: each party voluntarily extends into the shared space. A robot that drives the whole distance into the person's personal space risks reading as intrusive; one that does not move reads as passive. No data quantify this for toasts.
- Japanese kanpai etiquette of holding one's glass rim lower than a superior's implies that vertical position of the glass at contact encodes status; this is etiquette lore, not researched, but a robot designer must choose a height policy.

### Gaps
- No kinematic or observational peer-reviewed study of the toast gesture (trajectory, who moves further, contact speed, glass height by status) beyond the Eguchi & Fukui abstract.
- Embodied approach–avoidance effects of arm movements (Chen & Bargh 1999 style) were deliberately not relied on; that literature has known replication difficulties (not verified this session).
- No study of proxemic comfort when a robot extends a glass toward a seated/standing person.

## 6. Multisensory congruence: does simultaneity of seeing, hearing and feeling the contact matter?

### Takeaway
A real clink delivers visual, auditory and vibrotactile signals from one location within a few milliseconds; multisensory research shows such coincident signals are bound into one event only within limited temporal windows (tens to a few hundred ms, narrowest for sharp transients) and that sound actively changes what touch feels like. So a robot's clink should be physically real or tightly synchronized; but no study has tested multisensory congruence in a *social* contact event like a toast.

### Cited Findings
- [V] The temporal binding window is the range of asynchronies over which inputs from different senses are likely to be integrated; simple stimuli with sharp rise times yield **narrower** windows than complex stimuli (Stevenson, Zemtsov & Wallace 2012; Stevenson & Wallace 2013), and the audiovisual window is narrower for auditory-leading than visual-leading pairs. — [review, PMC3795069](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3795069); [PMC3526341](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3526341)
- [M] Tutorial review of intersensory synchrony perception (audio–visual–tactile), incl. just-noticeable asynchronies on the order of tens of ms for simple stimuli and temporal recalibration/ventriloquism. Vroomen, J., & Keetels, M. (2010). Perception of intersensory synchrony: A tutorial review. *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 72*(4), 871–884. — [doi:10.3758/APP.72.4.871](https://doi.org/10.3758/APP.72.4.871) (specific ms values not verified this session)
- [V] Audiotactile, audiovisual and visuotactile pairings differ: audiotactile and audiovisual temporal processing share mechanisms but are linked respectively to objective vs. subjective synchrony. Noel, J.-P., Wallace, M. T., Orchard-Mills, E., Alais, D., & Van der Burg, E. (2015). True and perceived synchrony are preferentially associated with particular sensory pairings. *Scientific Reports, 5*, 17467. — [doi:10.1038/srep17467](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4664927/)
- [M] Sound changes felt touch: amplifying/boosting high frequencies of hand-rubbing sounds makes skin feel drier/smoother ("parchment-skin illusion"). Jousmäki, V., & Hari, R. (1998). Parchment-skin illusion: sound-biased touch. *Current Biology, 8*(6), R190. — [doi:10.1016/S0960-9822(98)70120-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0960-9822(98)70120-4); extended by Guest, S., Catmur, C., Lloyd, D., & Spence, C. (2002). Audiotactile interactions in roughness perception. *Experimental Brain Research, 146*, 161–171. — [doi:10.1007/s00221-002-1164-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-002-1164-z) (DOIs unverified)
- [M] Sound changes perceived food/drink properties (crispness; carbonation). Zampini, M., & Spence, C. (2004). The role of auditory cues in modulating the perceived crispness and staleness of potato chips. *Journal of Sensory Studies, 19*(5), 347–363. — [doi:10.1111/j.1745-459x.2004.080403.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-459x.2004.080403.x)
- [V] Timing between partners' contributions affects how a jointly produced sound is processed (reduced sensory attenuation with larger inter-partner delay). Loehr (2013). — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00172](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00172/full)

### Inferences
- A clink is a sharp-onset transient — exactly the stimulus class with the narrowest binding windows — so synthetic substitutes (speaker clink, vibration motor) must be synchronized to within a few tens of ms and co-located with the visible contact, or the event will fragment into "a touch and, separately, a sound". The simplest way to satisfy this is real glass-on-glass contact.
- Because audition modulates touch, the *quality* of the sound (bright, ringing vs. dull thud) will likely colour how the impact feels (crisp/"glassy" vs. dead), and by extension how satisfying the toast feels. This is extrapolation from parchment-skin/crispness work.
- A clean, congruent see–hear–feel event is also the unambiguous *success signal* of the joint action; the social value may sit less in each modality than in their coincidence marking a shared moment. Speculative.

### Gaps
- No research on multisensory congruence in social contact rituals (handshake, high-five, clink) and its effect on closeness was found; "social touch congruency" studies concern stroking/vision of touch, not impacts, and were not reviewed here.
- No measured data on the audio/visual/tactile timing profile of a real clink or on tolerance for artificial delays.

### Inferences
- Tentative priority for a toasting robot (hypothesis, ordered by evidence strength × functional necessity):
  1. **Contingent gaze** (face → glasses → face) — best evidence, cheap, and the only cue with toast-specific support; moderate intensity for Japanese users.
  2. **Legible, mutually negotiated approach gesture** with correct timing — prerequisite for everything else; "meeting halfway" supports joint agency.
  3. **A real, well-timed physical clink producing congruent sound + vibration** — the unique signature of the ritual and the success signal of the joint action; evidence is by analogy only, but faking it risks multisensory incongruence.
  4. **Unison "kanpai!"** — culturally expected timing signal; bonding effect by weak analogy.
- The most defensible general claim is not that any one channel "creates connection", but that a toast is a small **joint action** whose successful, synchronous completion — signalled redundantly in gaze, sound, touch and voice — is what people experience as connection. This synthesis is the researcher's inference from the joint-action/synchrony literature; it has not been directly tested.
- The absence of direct studies means the robot project itself could produce novel evidence (e.g. factorial ablation of gaze / sound / contact / voice with closeness and joint-agency measures).

### Gaps
- No experiment anywhere (human–human or human–robot) was found that manipulates clink presence, clink sound, eye contact or unison call during a toast and measures closeness, trust or enjoyment.
- J-STAGE and sdu.dk were unreachable from the research sandbox, so the two most toast-specific sources (Eguchi & Fukui 2020; Fischer et al. 2020) are known only from abstracts/search summaries; exact statistics need checking. A broader Japanese-language search (CiNii/J-STAGE for 乾杯 with 同調・共同行為・一体感) found no further relevant papers but was shallow.
